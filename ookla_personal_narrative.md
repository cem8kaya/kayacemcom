# My Story — Interview Narrative
### Cem Kaya · Personal Background & Talking Points

---

## Who I Am — The Opening

I'm a telecom engineer who became obsessed with making data useful.

That's the simplest version. The longer version is that I've spent fifteen years inside telecommunications networks — not designing them on paper, but operating them, breaking them, fixing them, and eventually building software that understands them better than any human analyst could.

I started in voice networks. SIP, SBCs, IMS. The kind of infrastructure where a misconfiguration at two in the morning meant thousands of calls dropping across a country. That environment teaches you something that no classroom does: urgency, precision, and the understanding that every number in a dashboard represents a real person trying to reach someone.

That foundation never left me. Even now, when I'm writing Python or building an AI agent, I'm thinking about what's actually happening in the network — what the data means in terms of user experience, what a spike in session failure rate tells you about a misconfiguration three hops upstream. That combination — deep domain knowledge plus data engineering plus AI — is genuinely rare, and it's what I've been building toward, sometimes deliberately, sometimes by following the most interesting problem in the room.

---

## Where I Started

My early career was entirely operational. I worked at Turkcell, at Vodafone Turkey, ran my own consulting practice for a while doing VoIP deployments for carriers and enterprises. The work was concrete: keep the network running, resolve the incident, explain what happened.

What I noticed early on was that the same problems kept recurring. Not because the engineers were bad — they were excellent — but because there was no systematic way to learn from what the network was telling you. The data was there. The insight wasn't.

That observation became the thread that runs through everything I've done since.

---

## Turkcell — Where I Learned to Build

When I came back to Turkcell in 2019 as a Senior Core Network Optimization Engineer, I made a decision: instead of just analyzing network problems, I was going to build the tools that make the analysis automatic.

I built a network operations automation platform from scratch. Python, SQL, JavaScript — no vendor product, just code that understood how our specific network behaved. The system flagged anomalies before they became incidents, generated structured reports for operational teams, and reduced manual intervention by seventy percent. Those outputs went to executive dashboards. The people making strategic decisions about the network were, without knowing it, looking at something I had built.

The other major thing I did at Turkcell was lead the VoiceX migration — moving our IMS infrastructure from a legacy multi-vendor architecture of Ericsson and Huawei systems to a cloud-native Mavenir platform. This was a program that touched millions of VoLTE subscribers. We did it with zero service downtime. That kind of migration — where you're coordinating across engineering teams, vendors, and executive stakeholders simultaneously, and the margin for error is essentially zero — teaches you a different kind of skill. It's not just technical. It's about trust, communication, and the ability to hold a complicated situation together under pressure.

---

## B-YOND — Where I Learned to Work at Scale

B-YOND was a different kind of challenge. Suddenly I wasn't working on one operator's network — I was working on six of them simultaneously, across different countries, different architectures, different problem types. Deutsche Telekom, AT&T, Verizon, Telia Sonera, O2, Ooredoo. Each with their own network characteristics, their own internal politics, their own definition of what "root cause" means.

This is where I developed what I think of as my most transferable skill: the ability to walk into a complex technical environment I've never seen before, understand what the data is telling me, and communicate that understanding to people at every level of the organization — from the network engineer who needs to fix something tonight, to the VP who needs to explain what happened to the board next week.

The engagement that I talk about most is the FIFA World Cup 2023 with Ooredoo Qatar. Ooredoo was handling the connectivity for one of the most watched sporting events on the planet. The stakes were as visible as it gets in telecoms. I had built a real-time anomaly detection system for their network, and during the tournament it was the operational tripwire — the thing that would catch a problem before it became visible to users. We had zero critical incidents. Not because nothing happened — things always happen in a live network at that scale — but because we caught them early enough to act.

That experience crystallized something for me. The difference between good network operations and great network operations isn't the technology. It's the speed of understanding. How quickly can you go from a signal in the data to a clear picture of what's happening and what to do about it? Everything I've built since has been oriented toward making that speed faster.

---

## Odine Labs — Where I Explored the Frontier

When I joined Odine Labs in 2025, I shifted into a more exploratory mode. The company is building AI-native products for the telecom space, which meant I had permission to ask bigger questions.

I built multi-agent AI systems using LangGraph — systems where multiple specialized AI agents collaborate to diagnose network problems autonomously. I fine-tuned language models on telecom-specific data so they could understand 3GPP specifications and reason about network faults in the way a domain expert would. I built synthetic data generators for training ML models in scenarios you can't easily capture in production.

This work sits at the cutting edge of what AI can do for telecommunications. It also taught me something important: the operators who will benefit most from AI are the ones who have someone who understands both the AI and the domain deeply enough to bridge the gap. That person is hard to find. I've been intentional about becoming that person.

## What I'm Looking For — and Why Ookla

What I want next is a role where the work is about outcomes for customers, not just outputs from an engineering team.

My whole career has been oriented toward making complex things understandable and actionable. At B-YOND I did that inside customer engagements — but the primary identity was still "data scientist." What draws me to a Customer Solutions role is that the customer relationship becomes the primary product. The technical depth I bring isn't a nice-to-have. It's the thing that lets me have an honest conversation with a network engineering team, earn their trust, and then translate what I find into something their leadership can act on.

Ookla specifically is interesting to me for a few reasons.

First, the data. Speedtest has the largest crowdsourced connectivity dataset in the world. RootMetrics has the controlled benchmark data. Downdetector surfaces user-reported signals before operators' own monitoring systems catch them. Ekahau brings in the Wi-Fi dimension. The combination of those four datasets is genuinely unique — and nobody is fully exploiting the cross-dataset insights yet. That's an AI and analytics problem, which is exactly where I live.

Second, the position in the market. Ookla's data is trusted by operators precisely because it's independent. Operators use it for regulatory reporting, for marketing claims, for competitive positioning. But the much bigger opportunity is using it internally for network strategy. That conversation — moving from "we use Ookla for our press releases" to "we use Ookla to make our investment decisions" — is a conversation I know how to have with a technical and executive audience.

Third, and honestly, the timing. AI is changing what's possible in connectivity intelligence faster than most people in the industry realize. The operators who will lead the next decade are the ones who figure out how to turn data into decisions at speed. Ookla is positioned to enable that. I want to be part of building it.

## A Note on Wi-Fi

One thing I'm direct about: my deepest technical background is in cellular core networks, not Wi-Fi. Ekahau is a product I'm learning, not one I've shipped. I think it's important to say that clearly.

What I'd also say is this: someone who has operated at the 5G core network level — who understands QoS, session management, radio-to-core handoffs, and the full protocol stack — learns Wi-Fi architecture at a conceptual level very quickly. The principles transfer. And I've spent real time studying the Ekahau methodology and Ookla's product suite in preparation for these conversations, because I take the learning seriously.

The gap is real. My plan to close it is also real.

## How I Work

I'm a builder by instinct. When I see a problem that's being solved manually and repeatedly, my first thought is: what would it take to automate this? When I see data that nobody is looking at systematically, I want to know what it's hiding.

I work well in ambiguity. The best problems don't come with clear definitions, and the best solutions usually require someone who can sit with incomplete information long enough to form a genuine hypothesis rather than reaching for the most obvious answer.

I communicate at multiple levels without effort. I can write a 3GPP-compliant protocol parser in C++ in the morning and explain the business implications of a network anomaly to a CFO in the afternoon. That range is something I've developed deliberately, because I've seen too many technically excellent people fail to create impact because they couldn't make their findings land with the people who needed to act on them.

And I care about the outcome, not the output. The analysis isn't done when the model runs. It's done when someone makes a better decision because of it.
